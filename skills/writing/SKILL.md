# Writing

Write for the reader's decision or understanding. Preserve facts, intent,
and the author's voice. Plain writing can be warm, opinionated, and precise;
it does not need to sound sterile.

## Find the point

Identify the audience, purpose, and facts available. Infer them from context
when clear. Ask only when a missing fact changes the message. Lead with the
answer or outcome, then the explanation the reader needs to assess it.

For editing, preserve the author's meaning, uncertainty, terminology, and
valid quotations. Do not strengthen a claim, invent a number, or supply a
motivation just to make a sentence persuasive. Flag a factual gap instead.

## Draft with concrete language

- Name the actor, action, and result. Replace "improves reliability" with
  the mechanism or measured change when known.
- Prefer familiar words and direct verbs. Cut "it is important to note",
  "in order to", "leverage", and ceremonial introductions.
- Keep one main thought per sentence. Vary sentence length when it helps
  the rhythm. Use the same term for the same concept.
- Connect paragraphs in the order the reader needs the ideas. Include a
  technical detail only when it explains a choice or supports a claim.
- Use lists for parallel items or steps and tables for real comparisons.
  Use headings when length requires navigation, in sentence case. Avoid
  bold labels that repeat the sentence underneath them.
- Use plain punctuation. Prefer a period to a long aside. Avoid decorative
  emojis, repeated em dashes, canned contrasts, and forced sets of three.
- Match the user's tone. Use first person when appropriate. Give an opinion
  with its reason; do not invent personal experience or perform enthusiasm.

## Edit for evidence and voice

Read it once for the argument and once for the language. Remove claims
that could fit any project unchanged. Replace vague attribution with a
source or remove it. Distinguish observed facts, inference, and unresolved
uncertainty without hedging every sentence.

Cut promotional language, repetitive conclusions, empty reassurance, and
offers to do work already requested. Preserve necessary nuance; shorter
is useful only while meaning survives. Do not damage a quotation or required
format to satisfy a word or punctuation preference.

Return the requested text. Include editorial notes only for a substantive
meaning change or a factual question the reader needs to resolve. Do not
attach a checklist explaining how human the writing now sounds.
