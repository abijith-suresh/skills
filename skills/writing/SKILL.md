# Writing

Write something the intended reader can understand and use. Let the author's
meaning and voice guide the prose, rather than a template for sounding human.

## Workflow

### 1. Establish the brief

Use the request, supplied text, and surrounding context to identify the audience,
what they already know, the purpose, and the intended response. An explanation,
a request, and a personal reflection need different openings and levels of detail.
Respect the requested format, length, language, and degree of formality. Use an
author's writing sample when available to understand their voice.

Ask a focused question if a missing detail would materially change the message.
For routine gaps, make a reasonable choice and keep drafting. Do not turn every
writing task into an interview.

Apply this workflow within the authorized task. Loading it or any companion
skill does not authorize sending, publishing, or changing additional documents.
No other skill is required.

### 2. Secure the meaning

Before drafting, distinguish supplied facts, the author's judgments, and unknowns.
When editing, preserve names, numbers, dates, quotations, attribution, conditions,
and the strength of claims. Keep technical terms when they carry needed meaning.

Do not invent evidence, anecdotes, feelings, or personal experience to add voice.
Do not turn an estimate into a promise, a correlation into a cause, or an opinion
into a fact. Preserve disagreement and reservations rather than smoothing them
into enthusiasm or a neutral summary.

If sources conflict or an essential fact is missing, ask for clarification or mark
the gap in a working draft. Continue with unaffected passages. Surface unresolved
gaps separately when returning the draft; do not present it as ready to send.
Keep quotations intact unless the task explicitly permits changing them.

### 3. Shape the message

For a new draft, choose an opening and order that serve the reader's purpose.
For practical writing, usually lead with the answer, request, or result, then give
the context needed to understand it. A narrative may need a scene before its point.

For an edit, fix missing connections, buried points, and repetition before polishing
individual sentences. Keep useful passages that already work. A light edit should
not become a new argument or a rewrite of the author's personality.

Let each paragraph develop a point and connect it to the next. Use headings and
lists when they help readers navigate or compare information. Stop when the message
is complete; add a conclusion only if it resolves something or helps the reader act.

### 4. Make the language concrete and connected

Replace abstract praise and broad claims with the supported action, observation,
or consequence. Prefer familiar words and precise verbs. Name the actor when known
and relevant; retain passive voice when the actor is unknown or the outcome is the
focus. Explain unfamiliar terms without replacing accurate ones with vague synonyms.

Remove filler, repeated ideas, and introductions that announce what the text will
say. Keep qualifications that affect truth. Give a transition a real job, such as
showing cause, sequence, or a reservation.

Read adjacent sentences together. Split a sentence if its clauses obscure the
point; join short sentences when their relationship would otherwise be lost.
Repeat a key term when changing it would confuse the reader. Let sentence length
follow the thought rather than forcing a short-long pattern.

Match the author's stance and the situation. Warmth can come from attention to
the reader's needs; it does not require praise, jokes, slang, or an invented first
person. Judge words and punctuation by what they do in the passage. A word list,
readability score, or AI detector cannot settle an editorial decision.

### 5. Check and return the prose

Read the whole piece as its intended reader, then make a separate proofreading pass.

- Can the reader tell what matters and what to do or understand next?
- Does each claim still match the source, including uncertainty and exceptions?
- Does the piece still sound like this author addressing this audience?
- Do adjacent sentences connect without making the reader backtrack?
- Does each paragraph add needed information? Cut generic openings and endings.
- Are names, numbers, links, quotations, grammar, and formatting correct?

Return the requested draft or revision in its intended format. Add a brief note only
for material assumptions, unresolved facts, or substantive changes the user needs
to assess. Keep the editing process out of the finished prose unless requested.

## Before and after

These examples illustrate decisions, not phrases to copy. Details in the revisions
must already be supported by the task's material.

### State the action

Before: "We are pleased to announce the implementation of an enhancement to the
password recovery experience that enables users to reset their passwords by email."

After: "You can now reset your password by email."

This names the supported change for a user-facing announcement and removes the
announcement language that delays it.

### Keep the reservation

Before: "I believe this could potentially reduce support requests, but we have
not yet tested it with customers."

After: "I think this could reduce support requests, but we haven't tested it
with customers yet."

The edit removes stacked hedging while keeping the author's opinion and the
untested claim. "This will reduce support requests" would change the meaning.

### Connect the sentences

Before: "The file exceeded the size limit. The limit is 20 MB. This caused the
export to fail. You can split the file into smaller parts. You can try again."

After: "The export failed because the file exceeded the 20 MB limit. Split the
file into smaller parts and try again."

The explanation and next action belong together. The revision connects the
supplied cause and remedy without adding a new claim.

### Preserve the author's position

Before: "While the proposal offers several compelling benefits, I remain of the
opinion that the ongoing maintenance burden outweighs them."

After: "The proposal has benefits, but I think the maintenance it would require
outweighs them."

The objection stays an objection. Replacing it with "The proposal has pros and
cons" would erase the author's judgment.

### Keep a useful passive sentence

Before: "The backup was deleted at 14:10. We don't know who deleted it."

After: "The backup was deleted at 14:10. We don't know who deleted it."

The original is already clear. Naming an actor would invent a fact, and editing
for its own sake would not help the reader.

## Editorial grounding

Use these sources for deeper guidance when needed. This workflow adapts their
editorial principles; government tone and academic essay conventions do not apply
to every audience. The examples above are original.

- [Digital.gov: Principles of plain language](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/principles)
  informs the audience brief and organization around reader needs.
- [GOV.UK: Use clear language](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-language/)
  informs concrete wording, needed specialist terms, and useful passive voice.
- [UNC Writing Center: Revising drafts](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/revising-drafts/)
  informs checking purpose, organization, and evidence before editing sentences
  and proofreading.
